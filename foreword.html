Book Tiffany

Foreword

In more than forty-five years as a licensed administrator in senior care, I've witnessed nearly everything this profession can offer.

I've opened communities, rebuilt struggling operations, developed programs, navigated crises, and led organizations through times of tremendous growth and extraordinary challenge. I've spent decades serving seniors in executive and leadership roles across virtually every level of care.

But for all the professional experience I've accumulated, nothing has taught me more than being a son.

Today, my mother lives in an assisted living community and battles dementia. Like countless adult children, I've stood in that painful space between gratitude and grief, grateful she is still here, grieving the pieces of her that seem to drift further away with each passing season.

There is a particular heartbreak that comes with watching a parent change before your eyes. Their face is familiar. Their voice may still sound the same. Yet sometimes the person you've known your entire life feels just beyond reach. You find yourself desperately searching for moments of recognition, flashes of connection, fragments of who they once were.

No professional title prepares you for that.

And because I've lived this journey from both sides, from behind the administrator's desk and beside a parent's bed, I understand that the move into senior care is never simply a move.

It is a transition of identity.

It is a family's confrontation with aging, loss, uncertainty, and hope all at the same time.

Beneath the paperwork, assessments, regulations, medications, and care plans, something much deeper is happening. A son is worrying about his mother. A daughter is questioning whether she's making the right decisions. A husband is saying goodbye to the life he shared with his wife. A resident is wondering if anyone still sees the person they used to be.

This is where the real story of senior care lives.

And this is where Tiffany's story begins.

In all my years, I've met thousands of remarkable people.

I've worked alongside visionary entrepreneurs who changed the industry. I've hired literally thousands of caregivers whose compassion transformed lives. I've met innovators, leaders, advocates, and extraordinary human beings.

But until 2022, I had never met a Tiffany.

Some people enter a community as family members, friends or visitors. Tiffany entered as a student of the human experience.

What sets her apart is not simply her compassion or determination, it is her wisdom. She sees what others miss. Her perspective shifts your own. Watching her recognize patterns, connections, and needs that escape most people is like watching Oz Perlman the world-renowned mentalist, magician, author, and endurance athlete, at work.

What makes Tiffany truly unique is her rare combination of transformative talents.

First, she instinctively understands people. Second, she can redesign systems around the individual rather than forcing people to adapt to the system. Third, she has an uncanny ability to translate insight into practical action. Everything she brings to the table has tangible purpose even with intangible assets. That is why her Care Intelligence System is so powerful and why it works not only for families but also for care communities seeking to provide exceptional outcomes.

I first witnessed this when Tiffany moved her father, Don, into our community.

Don was a humble and wonderful man who was a decorated Air Force Colonel and bomber pilot with a life filled with extraordinary experiences.

When Tiffany moved her father into our community, she moved in too.

Not literally, of course. But in every other sense, she became fully immersed in the experience.

Unlike what I unfortunately see far too often, Tiffany did not simply find a facility, move furniture, unpack boxes, and visit once a week. She committed herself to understanding what it actually feels like when a parent wakes up in a care community after years of living independently and is suddenly confronting a major life transition that was not the choice and never part of the plan.

She learned how assisted living worked from the inside. She built genuine relationships with staff members. She befriended residents. She attended activities and events. She brought new ideas and energy into the community.

For more than two years, Tiffany visited and cared for her father nearly every day. Everyone in the building knew her.

Residents would wheel, scooter, and shuffle over to sit with Tiffany and Don. She listened to their stories with sincere interest and respect. More importantly, she saw them, not as elderly people defined by decline, but as the individuals they had always been.

She understood something profound: identity remains long after memory begins to fade.

I watched her meet residents exactly where they were, even as their cognitive states shifted from moment to moment. Residents who told her their life stories one day would not remember her the next. Their memory may have struggled, but their nervous systems clearly remembered the comfort, warmth, and authenticity she brought into every interaction.

What amazed me most was her ability to recognize the emotional and cognitive space someone occupied in the moment. She intuitively understood when a resident had mentally returned to childhood, to military service, to parenthood, or to another defining chapter of life.

Tiffany could identify it and track it and even help soothe someone excitable or emotional by understanding, at that moment, that the resident was transported back to when they were nine years old, all the while looking at someone who was ninety. This was quite a feat given the broad spectrum of cognitive decline and dementia with zero private or prior knowledge of a person beforehand. This is a vital skill because it informs those caring for someone how to calm and redirect them.

For someone with no formal medical training, psychology degree, or professional background in senior care specifically, her insights were extraordinary.

And they mattered.

Understanding where someone is emotionally and cognitively is one of the most effective ways to reduce distress, create connection, and improve quality of life for individuals experiencing dementia and cognitive decline.

As I'd walk past her and her dad, I would hear conversations about flying over the Philippines or Viet Nam, or a conversation that happened in a mess tent overseas with flying buddies, or one in a General's office that her dad was recounting as if it was happening in real time. The stories were extraordinary. Having gone through a similar journey with my own mother, I also recognized the quiet grief that accompanies dementia, the pain of watching someone you love gradually disappear while still sitting right in front of you.

In nearly five decades in this profession, I have never encountered an adult child with the level of devotion, attentiveness, and unwavering commitment that Tiffany demonstrated toward her father.

She anticipated his needs before he expressed them. She understood his emotions when he could no longer articulate them. She pursued his comfort, dignity, and well-being with relentless determination.

Yet her influence extended far beyond Don.

She befriended families and shared wonderful things with out of town sons and daughters about their mom or dad that brought such comfort. But this isn't about touting Tiffany as a fan favorite. The point is that she was present at every level. She immersed first. She sought to understand.

Tiffany became passionate about addressing what I call silent suffering, the loneliness, confusion, grief, and disconnection that often accompany aging and transitions in care. Her dedication to reconnecting seniors back to their familiar identities and her approaches to make that happen are revolutionary.

She saw social - emotional concepts that she wanted to integrate when her dad was here that would result in better stabilization for the resident based on her experience and observations. Because she had put in the time to know the residents, the building, and the business, I listened.

We brainstormed together.

Years earlier, I had developed a memory-enhancement and transition-support program called Transitions. As Tiffany and I combined our respective perspectives, hers from the family experience and mine from decades of professional practice - we expanded and refined those concepts.

The results were powerful.

The community improved. Residents benefited. Families felt more connected. And most importantly, it worked.

Care is complex from every angle.

We argued, we disagreed, we laughed, we experienced care crises together with her dad, and our building, residents, staff, and providers were all better off because of it.

When Don passed away, we missed them both.

So when Tiffany shared the concept for this book, I immediately recognized its value. Quite simply, it was one of the most important ideas I had encountered in years.

The Five Box Method for Moving Parents should be required reading for any adult child, caregiver, or family preparing for a care transition. Tiffany's Care Intelligence System offers a practical, compassionate, and innovative framework unlike anything else I have seen.

This book will help you understand not only how to move a parent, but how to preserve their identity, dignity, and sense of belonging throughout the journey.

And that may be the most important gift of all.

Charles Bloom

Executive Director

Serving seniors and families for nearly fifty years
